1969–1977 · Before the flag

Before the flag.

We don’t have rights, but we do have pride.

  1. 1969

    Stonewall uprising

    Police raided the Stonewall Inn in New York's Greenwich Village on June 28. Patrons and neighbors fought back, sparking nights of protest.

  2. 1970

    The first domestic Pride marches

    On Stonewall's first anniversary, marches and gatherings took place in New York, Chicago, Los Angeles and San Francisco.

  3. 1971

    Pride comes to Canada

    Toronto's Gay Day picnic on August 1; two years later, Vancouver, Winnipeg and four other cities held Canada's first Pride Week.

  4. 1972

    The first Pride in England

    London held England's first Pride march on July 1, 1972.

  5. 1977

    Renée Richards

    A New York Supreme Court ruling let Renée Richards, a transgender woman, compete in the women's US Open.

  6. 1977

    Harvey Milk elected

    Elected to San Francisco's Board of Supervisors, he became the first openly gay elected official in California.

1978 · The original

Where it all began.

Debuted June 25, 1978, at San Francisco’s Gay Freedom Day Parade. Designed by Gilbert Baker with the encouragement of Harvey Milk, each of its eight stripes carried a meaning.

Select a stripe to see what it stands for.

Evolution

From eight stripes to many.

  1. 1978–79

    Six stripes

    Pink dropped (fabric was scarce), then turquoise, so the colors split evenly; indigo became royal blue.

  2. 2017

    Philadelphia

    Black and brown stripes added for queer people of color.

  3. 2018

    Progress Pride

    Daniel Quasar's chevron: black and brown for communities of color, plus the trans flag's blue, pink and white.

  4. 2021

    Intersex inclusive

    Valentino Vecchietti added the intersex flag's yellow triangle and purple circle.

  5. Today

    A family of flags

    Communities design their own flags to name and celebrate their identities.

Gender identity

Who we are.

Select a stripe to see what it stands for.

Transgender

Monica Helms · 1999

For people whose gender identity differs from what's culturally expected based on the sex they were assigned at birth. It's an umbrella term that says nothing about who someone is attracted to, and it encompasses people at all stages of their transition—socially or medically.

Genderqueer

Marilyn Roxie · 2011

For people who reject fixed categories of gender and embrace its fluidity. Genderqueer people may see themselves as both male and female, neither, or entirely outside those categories.

Non-binary

Kye Rowan · 2014

For people who don't identify only as a man or a woman. They may be both, somewhere in between, or outside those categories entirely. It's also an umbrella for identities like agender, bigender and gender-fluid.

Sexual orientation

Who we love.

Select a stripe to see what it stands for.

Bisexual

Michael Page · 1998

For people attracted to more than one gender, though not necessarily at the same time, in the same way or to the same degree.

Pansexual

Shared online · 2010

For people who can be attracted to people of any gender. Some people use pansexual and bisexual interchangeably.

Lesbian

Emily Gwen · 2018

For women who are emotionally, romantically or sexually attracted to other women. Some non-binary people use the term for themselves too.

Gay men

Popularized online · 2019

For men who are emotionally, romantically or sexually attracted to other men. That includes trans and non-binary men, whom the flag's white stripe names directly.

1987–2026

From pride to policy.

Progress

1987–2004

  1. 1987

    Barney Frank comes out

    The Massachusetts congressman becomes the first member of Congress to come out voluntarily.

  2. 1994

    LGBTQ History Month

    Rodney Wilson, a high school history teacher in Missouri, founds the month, observed each October.

  3. 1999

    Pride Month

    President Bill Clinton proclaims June as Gay and Lesbian Pride Month.

  4. 2001

    The Netherlands

    The first country to legalize same-sex marriage.

  5. 2004

    Massachusetts

    The first US state to legalize same-sex marriage.

Victories

2011–2024

  1. 2011

    Don't Ask, Don't Tell repealed

    Lesbian, gay and bisexual people can serve openly in the US military.

  2. 2012

    Tammy Baldwin elected

    Wisconsin's Tammy Baldwin becomes the first openly gay person elected to the US Senate.

  3. 2012

    Mark Takano elected

    California's Mark Takano becomes the first openly LGBTQ Asian American in Congress.

  4. 2015

    Obergefell v. Hodges

    The Supreme Court legalizes same-sex marriage nationwide.

  5. 2016

    Stonewall National Monument

    President Barack Obama designates the Stonewall Inn area a national monument.

  6. 2020

    Bostock v. Clayton County

    The Supreme Court rules that the 1964 Civil Rights Act's ban on job discrimination covers sexual orientation and gender identity.

  7. 2020

    Ritchie Torres and Mondaire Jones elected

    The New York congressmen become the first openly gay Black members of Congress.

  8. 2024

    Sarah McBride elected

    Delaware's Sarah McBride becomes the first openly transgender person elected to Congress.

Setbacks

2018–2026

  1. 2018

    Setback

    Military ban reinstated

    President Trump's ban on transgender service members is issued; it takes effect in 2019, and a new ban follows in 2025.

  2. 2025

    Setback

    "One Flag" policy

    A State Department policy bars Pride flags at US embassies.

  3. 2025

    Setback

    Gender-affirming care

    27 states restrict gender-affirming care for minors; the Supreme Court upholds Tennessee's ban.

  4. 2026

    Setback

    Stonewall's flag

    The Park Service removes the Pride flag from Stonewall National Monument; it's restored in April after a lawsuit.

  5. 2026

    Setback

    Attacks on LGBTQ+ youth

    The Supreme Court upholds trans sports bans and rules that talk-therapy conversion bans face strict First Amendment scrutiny.

Intersectionality

The parts of who we are overlap — and create experiences all their own.

The LGBTQ+ community gives people language for their intersecting identities and a place to celebrate them together, not in isolation. We aren’t free of prejudice, so we keep learning about each other, stay open to new ideas, and fight for the liberation of the whole community.

Just for fun · Flagging

Signals in plain sight.

Green carnation

Oscar Wilde had friends wear them to his 1892 premiere, and they became a quiet signal.

“Friend of Dorothy”

1930s–40s slang for a gay man. Dorothy may be Oz’s heroine, or Dorothy Parker.

The hanky code

In the 1970s, a colored hanky in a back pocket said you were gay, and what you were into.

Right ear piercing

A 1980s–90s rule of thumb said a stud in the right ear meant a man was gay.

The key code

Lesbians clipped keys or a carabiner to a belt loop to signal their orientation. The side mattered.
